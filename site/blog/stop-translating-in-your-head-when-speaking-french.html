How to stop translating in your head when you speak French

If you build every French sentence in English first and then translate it, you will always be a beat late. This guide explains why that happens, what research says about it, and a routine that moves you towards speaking French more directly. It is the approach we use at Fluency AI.

Key takeaways

  • Some first-language activation is automatic, even in fluent bilinguals, so the aim is to depend on it less, not to switch it off.[1]
  • Learning ready-made phrases made learners sound more proficient in a small experiment.[2]
  • Practise by recalling a phrase from a situation, not from an English prompt.[3]
  • Say phrases aloud in short chunks, a little every day.[4]

Why you translate in your head

Translating is not a sign that you are doing something wrong. In one study of 15 Chinese–English bilinguals who learned English after the age of 12, brain recordings showed that their first language was activated when they read English words, without their knowing it. The same pattern appeared in a listening task.[1] The task was comprehension, not speaking, and it was a single study. Even so, it fits what many learners report: your first language does not go quiet just because you want it to.

So the useful question is not “how do I stop translating?” but “how do I stop needing translation to build a sentence?” That is our interpretation of the research, and the answer is to store French in larger pieces.

Build French in chunks, not word by word

In one small experiment, 32 college students spent 22 teaching hours with listening and reading materials. The experimental group (17 students) was taught to notice standard word combinations, while the control group (15) was not. Two blind judges then rated the experimental group’s oral interviews as more proficient.[2] It was a small-scale study, but it supports a practical idea: a repertoire of ready-made phrases lets you speak without assembling each sentence from single words.

A four-step routine

  1. Learn each phrase with a scene, not an English word. Write the French chunk and a short note about where you would say it, such as « à la boulangerie » or “asking a colleague’s opinion”. After the first time you check the meaning, stop looking at the English.
  2. Recall from the scene (5 minutes). Cover the French, read only your scene notes, and say the phrase aloud. Then check. Students who tested themselves remembered more than students who re-read, in a study that used prose passages rather than languages, so we apply the idea by analogy.[3]
  3. Say it in chunks, out loud (5 minutes). Pick a short clip of clear French and speak along with it, repeating phrases rather than single words. In an eight-week study of 16 learners, shadowing short dialogues improved all the speaking measures except accentedness.[4]
  4. Describe your surroundings for 60 seconds. Do not stop and do not switch to English. If a word is missing, talk around it with « c’est un truc qui sert à… » (it’s a thing used for…) or « c’est comme… » (it’s like…). Our article on why we freeze in a new language has more rescue phrases.

Questions people ask

Is it normal to keep translating even when you are fluent?

Yes. In the study we cite, the participants were fluent bilinguals who had learned English after the age of 12, and their first language was still activated without their awareness.[1] That study looked at comprehension, so treat it as a reason to be patient with yourself rather than a measure of your speaking.

Should I stop using a bilingual dictionary?

No. Use it to look a word up, then store the French phrase with a scene, as in step one below, so that next time you recall the phrase and not the English word. As you reach higher levels, a French-French learner’s dictionary is a useful next step. This is our suggestion rather than a research finding.

How long until I stop translating?

We cannot promise a timeline, and the aim is to depend on translation less rather than to remove it. Measure it instead: time how long you take to answer a simple question in French in week one, and again in week four.
