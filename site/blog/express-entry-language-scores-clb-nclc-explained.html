CLB and NCLC explained: the language scores you need for Express Entry

Almost every Express Entry question about language comes back to two acronyms: CLB and NCLC. Your IELTS band, CELPIP level, TEF score or TCF score means nothing to the system until it has been converted into one of them. This guide shows the conversion, the minimums, and what each step is worth.

Read this first. This is general information, not immigration advice, and Fluency AI is not an immigration adviser or an exam body. Every figure below comes from IRCC pages dated 16 October 2026.[1][2] Rules and charts change, so check the linked IRCC pages before you book a test or enter scores in your profile.

Key takeaways

  • IRCC measures English with the Canadian Language Benchmarks (CLB) and French with the NCLC, one level per ability: speaking, listening, reading and writing.[1]
  • The Federal Skilled Worker Program needs level 7 in all four abilities. The Canadian Experience Class needs 7 for TEER 0 or 1 jobs and 5 for TEER 2 or 3 jobs.[1]
  • For a single applicant, each ability is worth 17 CRS points at CLB 7 and 31 at CLB 9: 68 versus 124 points across four abilities.[2]
  • For TEF Canada, use the “Équivalence ancien score” column. Results must be under two years old at profile and at application.[1]

What CLB and NCLC are

The Canadian Language Benchmarks describe English ability on a numbered scale, and the NCLC is the French equivalent.[4] You cannot sit a “CLB exam”. Instead you take an approved test, and IRCC converts each of your four results into a CLB or NCLC level using its own tables.[1]

Two details matter more than they first appear. First, the conversion is per ability, so you end up with four levels, not one overall score. Second, program minimums apply to all four, so your weakest ability decides whether you qualify.

The TEF figures are the “Équivalence ancien score” values, the column IRCC tells you to use. TCF Canada reports speaking and writing on a short scale (10–11 for NCLC 7) and listening and reading on a long one.[1]

How language becomes CRS points

Once you are eligible, your Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) score decides your place in the pool. Language is scored per ability, and the first official language can earn up to 136 points for a single applicant (128 with an accompanying spouse or partner).[2]

Moving from level 7 to level 9 in all four abilities is worth 56 points on this table alone (124 minus 68) for a single applicant. The four-ability totals are our arithmetic from IRCC’s per-ability figures.[2] And the benefit does not stop there:

  • Skill transferability. Strong language combined with a post-secondary degree, or with foreign work experience, earns extra points. Reaching level 9 in all four abilities doubles the amount available in those combinations, from a maximum of 25 to a maximum of 50 each, within a section capped at 100.[2]
  • A second official language. Reaching level 9 or more in all four abilities of your second language adds up to 24 points for a single applicant.[2]
  • French bonus. NCLC 7 or higher in all four French abilities adds 25 additional points, or 50 if you also reach CLB 5 or higher in all four English abilities.[2]
Heads-up on what is changing. IRCC states that from 3 November 2026 it is changing how points are awarded for Canadian work experience and valid job offers.[2][3] That affects other parts of the CRS, not the language tables above, but check the “What has changed” page before you estimate a total score.

Five mistakes that cost candidates

  1. Taking the wrong version of a test. IELTS Academic is not accepted; IELTS General Training is.[1]
  2. Entering the wrong TEF column. Use “Équivalence ancien score”, not “Score / 699”.[1]
  3. Letting results expire. They must be under two years old at both profile completion and application. Expired results mean refusal.[1]
  4. Ignoring the weakest ability. A strong average hides a failing ability, and minimums apply to all four. After a practice test, find your lowest skill and train that one.
  5. Aiming only for the minimum. Level 7 qualifies you; level 9 can change your ranking. Decide your target before you book, because retaking costs time and money.

Choosing a test

IRCC treats the accepted tests as equivalent once converted, so the best test is the one on which you score highest and can actually book. A few practical differences to weigh:

  • Language. French results can add CRS points even alongside strong English, as shown above.[2]
  • Format. CELPIP-General is computer-based with eight speaking tasks. IELTS General Training has an examiner-led speaking interview in three parts, and the Task 1 writing is a letter. TCF Canada’s speaking test lasts about twelve minutes (interview, role-play, opinion). Choose the format you find least stressful.
  • Availability. Check test centres and dates in your country with the provider before you plan your timeline.[5]

Preparing for the speaking and writing tasks

Most candidates know the language rules; fewer know the task formats. A sensible plan:

  1. Take one timed practice test to find your weakest ability.
  2. Learn each task’s format, time limit and marking criteria.
  3. Rehearse speaking out loud, not in your head, with a partner or a speaking coach.
  4. Write to the time and word limits, then compare against a model.

Questions people ask

Is IELTS Academic accepted for Express Entry?

No. IRCC requires the IELTS General Training option, and it does not accept IELTS One Skill Retake for Express Entry.[1]

Which TEF Canada score do I enter in my profile?

Enter the scores from the “Équivalence ancien score” (previous score equivalency) column of your statement of results. Do not enter the “Score / 699” results, which IRCC says are not compatible with its system. Entering the wrong scores can lead to a refused application.[1]

How long are my language results valid?

They must be less than two years old when you complete your Express Entry profile and again when you submit your application for permanent residence. Expired results lead to a refusal.[1]

Can French help even if my English is strong?

Yes. The CRS adds points for French: 25 if you score NCLC 7 or higher in all four French abilities and CLB 4 or lower in English (or did not take an English test), and 50 if you also reach CLB 5 or higher in all four English abilities.[2]
