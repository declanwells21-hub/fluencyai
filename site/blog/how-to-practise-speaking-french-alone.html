How to practise speaking French alone, in 20 minutes a day

You do not need a conversation partner to improve your spoken French. You need short, regular practice that makes you say French out loud, plus a way to check yourself. This is the 20-minute daily routine we suggest at Fluency AI, with the research behind each step.

Key takeaways

  • Practise out loud for 20 minutes a day in four blocks of five: shadow, recall, talk, record.
  • In an eight-week study, learners who practised shadowing short dialogues improved on all speaking measures except accentedness. It was a small study.[1]
  • Saying things from memory beats re-reading them for long-term retention in lab studies, so we practise by recalling, not by rereading.[2]
  • Without a partner, recording yourself is your feedback.

Why practising alone works, and where it falls short

Speaking is a skill, and skills improve by doing. In one study, sixteen learners practised shadowing short dialogues on iPods for eight weeks. They improved significantly on all the speaking measures except accentedness, and the authors point out that shadowing can be done outside a classroom at minimal cost.[1] It is a small study, so treat it as encouraging rather than conclusive.

Retrieval matters too. Students who tested themselves on a text remembered more after two days and a week than students who re-read it, even though re-reading made them feel more confident.[2] That study used prose passages, not French, so we apply the principle by analogy: say it from memory instead of looking at it again.

Timing matters as well. A meta-analysis of 839 assessments from 317 experiments found that spacing study sessions apart affects what is remembered later, and that the best gap depends on how long you need to remember something.[3] Those experiments tested verbal memory rather than speaking, so we use them as a design guide: several short sessions a week, not one long session. Low-stakes practice matters too, because a meta-analysis of second language anxiety found that more anxious learners tended to achieve less, with a moderate rather than overwhelming link.[4]

The weakness of practising alone is that nobody corrects you. The routine below builds in checks to make up for that.

The 20-minute routine

  1. Shadow (5 minutes). Choose a 30 to 60 second clip of clear French. Listen once. Then play it again and speak along a fraction of a second behind, copying the rhythm and melody as well as the words. Repeat three times. Short clips keep this manageable.
  2. Recall out loud (5 minutes). Close your notes. Say the five sentences or phrases you learned yesterday, then check them. Anything you miss goes on tomorrow’s list.[2]
  3. Talk without stopping (5 minutes). Set a timer for 60 seconds and describe your day or your street in French without stopping. If you stall, use a rescue phrase such as « Comment dire… ? » or « Je veux dire que… » and keep going. Do it twice and try to say it better the second time. Our article on why we freeze in a new language explains why rescue phrases help.
  4. Record and fix one thing (5 minutes). Record 60 seconds on your phone. Listen back once and choose one fix: a sound, a verb ending, a missing word. Write it down and start tomorrow’s recall block with it.

Choosing your material

Pick audio you can understand at least half of at first listening. If it is much harder, you will spend the five minutes confused instead of practising; if it is much easier, you will not be stretched. Reuse the same clip for the whole week, because repeating familiar material is what turns it into speech you can produce, and change it on Monday. On weekends, skip the routine or replace it with a single longer recording of two or three minutes. Keep a one-line log of what you practised and your one fix. It takes under a minute and shows your progress over time. These choices are our suggestions, built on the research above, and you should adjust them to your level and your week.

Closing the feedback gap

A recording is only useful if you compare it with something. Play your attempt next to the model clip from block one and listen for differences in rhythm and the sounds you find hardest. Then add a human check once a week: a teacher, a French-speaking friend or a language exchange partner who listens to one recording and tells you one thing to fix.

If you are preparing for an exam, know what a strong answer looks like before you practise. Our short exam guides explain what each speaking task asks for, and our DELF oral practice guide shows how to apply this routine to B1 and B2.

Three mistakes to avoid

  • Practising silently. Reading French in your head trains reading. Say it aloud, even quietly.
  • Only repeating what you already know. Keep a small amount of new material in every session, perhaps one in five items, so the routine keeps stretching you. This is our suggestion rather than a research finding.
  • Skipping the recording because you dislike your voice. It is the only objective feedback you have when practising alone.

Questions people ask

Can a complete beginner practise speaking alone?

Yes, with smaller steps. Shadow a very short clip, such as a greeting or a single sentence, and recall three phrases instead of five. The routine works at any level because each block is about saying things aloud, not about how much French you already know.

How long until I notice a difference?

We cannot promise a timeline, and results vary. The shadowing study we cite ran for eight weeks, so give any routine a few weeks before you judge it.[1] A better test than a feeling is your own recordings: compare a 60-second recording from week one with one from week four.

Do I need a native speaker for the audio?

You need clear, natural French that is not too fast. A short news bulletin made for learners, a podcast excerpt or a slow dialogue all work. Choose clips you can understand at least in part, and keep them short so you can repeat them.
