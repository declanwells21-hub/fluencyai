IELTS General Training speaking: how the test works and how to practise each part

If you are applying to Canada through Express Entry with English, the IELTS General Training speaking test is one of four abilities that set your CLB level. This guide explains how the 11 to 14 minute test works, how it is marked, which speaking scores match CLB 7, 8 and 9, and how to practise each part. It builds on our guide to language scores for Express Entry, and on the speaking approach we use at Fluency AI.

Key takeaways

  • The speaking test is a face-to-face interview of 11 to 14 minutes in three parts, and it is recorded.[1][2][6]
  • Four criteria count equally: fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation.[2]
  • For Express Entry, IRCC maps speaking 6.0 to CLB 7, 6.5 to CLB 8 and 7.0 to CLB 9.[5]
  • The speaking test is the same in General Training and Academic, but IRCC accepts only General Training.[4][5]

How the speaking test is built

You speak one-to-one with a certified examiner, and the whole test lasts 11 to 14 minutes.[2]

The three parts of the IELTS speaking test
PartTimeWhat happens
1. Introduction and interview4 to 5 minutesQuestions about yourself and familiar topics such as home, family, work, studies and interests. If your answers are too short, the examiner may ask “why?” or “why not?”.[2][6]
2. Individual long turn3 to 4 minutesYou get a task card, a pencil and paper, and one minute to prepare. You then speak for one to two minutes, and the examiner asks one or two questions on the same topic.[3]
3. Two-way discussion4 to 5 minutesFurther questions linked to the Part 2 topic, moving to more abstract ideas and issues.[2]

How it is marked

Certified examiners assess you throughout the test on four criteria, and each carries the same weight.[1][2] Your result is a band from 0 to 9.[7]

  • Fluency and coherence: how well you speak at a normal pace without too much hesitation, and how clearly your ideas connect.[1]
  • Lexical resource: the range and precision of your vocabulary.
  • Grammatical range and accuracy: the variety of structures you use, and how accurately.
  • Pronunciation: how easy you are to understand.

The practical lesson is that no single skill can carry you. A learner with accurate grammar who pauses constantly loses on fluency, and a fast speaker with limited vocabulary loses on lexical resource.

The speaking score Express Entry needs

IRCC’s conversion tables map your IELTS General Training speaking band to a CLB level. The speaking figures are 6.0 for CLB 7, 6.5 for CLB 8 and 7.0 for CLB 9.[5] Each ability is converted separately, so a strong speaking score does not make up for a weak writing score, and the other three abilities have their own tables. IRCC also specifies the General Training version and does not accept the IELTS One Skill Retake for Express Entry.[5] Tables and rules change, so check IRCC’s current page, and see our full guide to CLB and NCLC scores for the whole picture.

How to practise each part

Part 1: answer, reason, example. Practise giving three short sentences instead of one: your answer, a reason, and a small example. This keeps you from the one-word answers that prompt “why?”. Rehearse common topics aloud: home, work, studies, family and free time.

Part 2: use the minute for keywords. Task cards list points to cover and ask you to explain something.[3] Spend the preparation minute writing four keywords, not sentences, then speak for the full one to two minutes. Practise with a timer set for three minutes: one to prepare and two to speak. If you run out of ideas, add a detail, an example or how you felt, and keep going.

Part 3: opinion, reason, other side. Part 3 moves to abstract questions, so practise a simple shape: give an opinion, support it with a reason, and acknowledge another view. Phrases that help include “it depends on…”, “one reason is that…”, “for example…” and “that said…”.

Every week: one full mock test. Record all three parts back to back, which should take about 11 to 14 minutes. Listen once and score yourself on the four criteria, then pick one thing to fix next week. If nerves are the main problem, read our article on why we freeze when we speak a new language.

Questions people ask

Is the speaking test different for General Training and Academic?

No. IDP states that the Speaking test is the same for both versions.[4] The difference that matters for Canada is which version you book: IRCC specifies the General Training option, not Academic, for Express Entry.[5]

Can I retake just the speaking part?

IRCC does not accept the IELTS One Skill Retake for Express Entry, so a single-skill retake will not help your Express Entry profile.[5] Check IRCC’s current language-test page before you book, because rules can change.

Is the speaking test recorded?

Yes. IDP states that each speaking test is conducted face to face with an examiner and recorded in case further review is necessary.[6] Treat it like a normal conversation and do not worry about the recording.
