French speaking practice for the DELF exam: how to prepare the B1 and B2 oral

The DELF oral is the one part of the exam you cannot cram from a page. This guide explains how the B1 and B2 oral exams are built, how they count towards the pass mark, and how to practise each part on your own. If you are still choosing an exam, start with our guide to the French levels France asks for.

Read this first. Fluency AI is not affiliated with France Éducation international, the public body that runs the DELF. Formats are revised from time to time, so check the official sample papers for the version you will sit.

Key takeaways

  • The B1 oral has three parts and lasts about 15 minutes, with 10 minutes of preparation for the third part only.[1]
  • The B2 oral has two parts, lasts 20 minutes and comes with 30 minutes of preparation.[3]
  • To pass you need 50 out of 100 and at least 5 out of 25 in every skill, so the oral cannot be neglected.[1]
  • Practise by speaking from keywords, not from a script.

How the oral counts towards the diploma

The DELF has four skills, each marked out of 25. To get the diploma you need at least 50 points out of 100, and a mark below 5 out of 25 in any one skill is eliminatory.[1] That second rule is the one candidates forget. A strong written exam does not rescue an oral that collapses, so treat speaking as a full quarter of the diploma from your first week of preparation.

The B1 oral, part by part

At B1 the oral is an individual exam of about 15 minutes with three exercises. You only get preparation time for the third one.[1][4]

The three parts of the DELF B1 oral, tout public
PartTimePreparationWhat you do
1. Entretien dirigé (guided interview)2 to 3 minutesNoneYou answer the examiner’s questions about yourself and your life.
2. Exercice en interaction (role play)3 to 4 minutesNoneYou play the role you are given and deal with a situation with the examiner.[2]
3. Expression d’un point de vue5 to 7 minutes10 minutesYou draw two subjects, choose one, and present your point of view from a document.[2]

The B2 oral

At B2 the oral has two parts, a sustained monologue and an interaction exercise. It lasts 20 minutes, and you get 30 minutes of preparation.[3] The longer preparation tells you what the exam expects: a structured, argued talk rather than a series of short answers.

How to practise each part on your own

Part 1: build stock answers. Write six topics on cards: yourself, your studies or work, your family, your free time, your city and your plans. Say a 30-second answer to each, out loud, then repeat without the cards. The goal is fluency in familiar territory so that your nerves are spent on the harder parts.

Part 2: practise reacting. There is no preparation, so rehearse the language of problems and solutions instead of fixed answers. Say the phrases below aloud in made-up situations, such as a cancelled booking or a noisy neighbour.

Useful French phrases for the interaction and point-of-view exercises
PurposeFrenchEnglish
Ask politelyPourriez-vous m’expliquer… ?Could you explain… to me?
ProposeJe vous propose de…I suggest that we…
Disagree politelyJe comprends, mais…I understand, but…
Give an opinionÀ mon avis… / Selon moi…In my opinion…
StructureD’une part… d’autre part… Pour conclure…On the one hand… on the other… To conclude…

Part 3 and the B2 monologue: plan, then speak from keywords. Use the preparation time for a skeleton, not a script: what the document says, your opinion, two arguments with an example each, and a conclusion. Write keywords only, because ten minutes is too short to write out a talk of five to seven minutes. Then practise speaking from the keywords with a timer. Retrieving ideas from memory, instead of rereading them, is what helps them stick, although the study behind that finding used prose passages rather than languages.[5]

Every week: one full mock oral. Record yourself doing all the parts back to back, listen once, and choose one thing to fix. Our guide on practising French alone has a 20-minute daily routine that fits around this.

Questions people ask

What score do I need in the oral to pass the DELF?

There is no separate pass mark for the oral, but there is a floor: a mark below 5 out of 25 in any one skill is eliminatory, and you also need 50 out of 100 overall.[1] In practice, aim well above 5 so that the oral supports your total instead of putting the diploma at risk.

Is the oral the same in the tout public, scolaire and junior versions?

Not necessarily. The official B1 manual covers three versions, tout public, scolaire and junior, so make sure you read the one that matches your registration.[1] This guide describes the tout public version.

Can I use DELF B1 as proof of my French level for a residence card?

A State prefecture page lists a DELF at A2 or B1 among the accepted proofs for the residence cards, depending on the level required. We explain the details, including validity, in our guide to the French levels required in France in 2026.
