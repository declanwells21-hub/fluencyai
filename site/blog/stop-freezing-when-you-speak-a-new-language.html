Why you freeze when you speak a new language, and 7 ways to stop

You know the word. You practised the sentence on the bus. Then someone at a café counter asks, “For here or to go?” and your mind goes completely blank.

If that sounds familiar, you are in very large company. Researchers have been studying this exact freeze for forty years, and what they have found is more reassuring, and more useful, than “just relax”.

Key takeaways

  • Freezing in a new language is a recognised, well-studied effect called foreign language anxiety. It is not a sign that you are “bad at languages”.[1]
  • Across 105 samples and almost 20,000 learners, higher anxiety went with lower achievement (an average correlation of −0.36). That is a real but moderate link, not a verdict on you.[2]
  • What helps most is more low-stakes speaking: short, private, repeatable practice, plus a few rescue phrases for the moment you stall.
  • Early studies of AI speaking partners look promising for comfort and willingness to talk, but are mixed on measurable speaking gains.[3][4][5]

What the freeze actually is

In 1986, Elaine Horwitz, Michael Horwitz and Joann Cope argued that anxiety in language classes deserves to be treated as its own phenomenon, not just general nervousness. They described three ingredients: communication apprehension (worry about speaking with other people), test anxiety, and fear of negative evaluation (worry about how others will judge you). They also built a 33-item questionnaire, the Foreign Language Classroom Anxiety Scale, which researchers still use.[1]

That split is more than academic trivia, because each ingredient points to a different fix:

Which fear matches which situation

Does it really hold you back?

A 2019 meta-analysis pooled 97 reports covering 105 independent samples and 19,933 learners from 23 countries. Across 216 effect sizes, the average correlation between second language anxiety and language achievement was r = −0.36.[2] In plain terms: more anxious learners tended to achieve less, and the link was moderate rather than overwhelming. The authors also found that the strength of the link varied with the type of achievement measure, the learners’ educational level, the target language and the type of anxiety.[2]

Two cautions keep this in proportion:

  • Correlation is not causation. Anxiety can hurt performance, and struggling can raise anxiety. Most researchers expect both.
  • An average is not you. Plenty of anxious learners reach a high level. The practical message is that reducing the fear is worth doing, not that you are stuck with it.

Seven ways to stop freezing

These are practical suggestions informed by the research above. They are coaching tips, not clinical treatment, and none of them is a magic switch. Try two or three rather than all seven at once.

1. Work out which fear you are dealing with

Use the table above. If your freeze is about strangers, do not spend a week on exam templates. If it is about exams, do not just chat more. Naming the ingredient tells you where to put your practice time.

2. Shrink the audience first

Fear of negative evaluation needs an evaluator. Remove the audience and the fear loses its fuel. Speak out loud on your own, record a voice note and listen back, or use a private partner such as a voice-based AI coach before you try a live conversation. The goal is to get the words moving through your mouth many times where nobody can judge them.

3. Practise little and often

Short, frequent sessions are easier to start than a heroic hour you will dread. A useful rule of thumb is two minutes of real speaking a day; in Fluency AI a talk-streak day counts when you speak for two minutes. That threshold is a design choice and a practical rule of thumb, not a research finding, but it works because it is small enough to do on a bad day.

4. Memorise four rescue moves

Most freezes are not caused by missing vocabulary. They are caused by having no way out of the silence. Learn four phrases until they come out automatically: ask them to repeat, ask them to slow down, ask them to explain differently, and ask for a word.

Four rescue phrases in French, German and English
MoveFrenchGermanEnglish
Say that againPouvez-vous répéter, s’il vous plaît ?Könnten Sie das bitte wiederholen?Sorry, could you say that again?
Slower, pleasePlus lentement, s’il vous plaît.Können Sie bitte langsamer sprechen?Could you speak a bit more slowly?
Explain differentlyPouvez-vous dire cela autrement ?Können Sie das anders erklären?Could you explain that another way?
Give me a wordComment dit-on… ?Wie sagt man … auf Deutsch?What is the word for…?

5. Aim to be understood, not perfect

Perfectionism is the engine of fear of negative evaluation. Change the target: the conversation comes first and the correction second. If you say “Can you explain me the menu?”, the waiter understood you and the conversation worked. Fix it afterwards (“Can you explain the menu to me?”) and move on. Saying it badly first and getting it right second keeps you talking, and talking is what builds fluency.

6. Rehearse the situation, not the vocabulary

Lists of words do not prepare you for a tired waiter talking at native speed. Rehearse whole situations instead: ordering at a café, phoning to rebook an appointment, a job interview, a visa appointment. Say both sides out loud if you have to. When the real moment arrives, it will feel like a repeat.

7. If an exam is the stress, rehearse the format

Test anxiety shrinks when the format stops being a surprise. Know how many tasks there are, how long you get to prepare, and what the examiner will ask, then practise under the same limits. What about practising with an AI partner?

This is a young research area, so it is worth being precise about what the evidence does and does not say:

  • A 2024 study in System compared different conversational generative AI chatbots with EFL learners. It reported that visually embodied chatbots improved the emotional experience of learning, while pre- and post-test speaking scores did not differ significantly between groups.[3]
  • A 2026 survey study found that more AI-assisted spoken English was associated with lower speaking anxiety and higher willingness to communicate with humans. The design is correlational, so it shows an association rather than proof that the practice caused the change.[4]
  • A 2026 longitudinal study of Spanish learners found delayed and differentiated effects, mainly in reduced boredom and stronger willingness to communicate.[5]

Our reading: a private AI partner looks like a sensible stepping stone. It lowers the cost of speaking, which is exactly what anxious learners need. It is not a replacement for talking to people, and the studies so far are short and mostly with university students, so treat the results as encouraging rather than settled.

A 7-day starter plan

Five minutes a day is enough to test whether this works for you. Pick one language and one everyday situation.

Seven-day speaking starter plan
DayFive-minute task
1Record a 60-second self-introduction. Do not re-record it.
2Learn the four rescue phrases. Say each five times, out loud.
3Role-play ordering food or a drink, both sides.
4Record your introduction again and compare it with day 1.
5Role-play a phone call or an appointment.
6Talk for two minutes about your day. Use a rescue phrase at least once.
7Say one real sentence to one real person.

When it is more than nerves

Everyone gets nervous speaking a new language. If your anxiety is intense, spreads well beyond language practice, or interferes with daily life, talk to a teacher, a doctor or a mental health professional. A speaking-practice tool, ours included, is for building skill and confidence. It is not a treatment.

Questions people ask

Is it normal to freeze even when I know the words?

Yes. Knowing a word and producing it in real time under social pressure are different skills, and the freeze is the kind of reaction foreign language anxiety research was built to describe.[1] Anxiety taxes attention, so the word you know is the first thing to slip.

Can practising with an AI replace speaking to real people?

No, and it does not need to. Early studies suggest a private AI partner can make speaking feel safer and raise willingness to talk, while measurable speaking gains are less consistent.[3][5] Use it as a rehearsal room, then speak to a person as soon as you are ready.

What should I do in the exact moment I freeze?

Do not try to recover the perfect sentence. Use a rescue phrase (“Sorry, could you say that again?”), buy a second with a filler, or say it in a simpler way. Being understood matters more than being elegant.

Should I correct myself mid-sentence?

Usually not while you are still building confidence. Finish the thought, let the other person respond, and fix the mistake afterwards. Constant self-correction feeds the fear of being judged.
